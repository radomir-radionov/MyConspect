### Interview preparation

**When you apply for the interview than you automatic got a homework**

- learn about the company
- repeat all that you should know

Before going into any interview, try to identify the weakest aspects of your candidacy from this company’s point of view. Then prepare the best answer you possible can to shore up your defenses.

**Do you have any questions for us?**

- What do you like best about working in your company?
- are your company made a course for learning english for their developers from b1 to b2 and beyond.
- How much time do you spend at work during the week?

**Sources:**

[https://novoresume.com/career-blog/interview-questions-and-best-answers-guide](https://novoresume.com/career-blog/interview-questions-and-best-answers-guide)

**Describe your ideal company, location and job**

- **Innovation and creativity:** An ideal IT company is one that encourages and fosters innovation and creativity. It is a company that is open to new ideas and is constantly seeking ways to improve its products and services.
- **Collaborative work environment:** An ideal IT company values collaboration and teamwork. It is an environment where employees feel comfortable sharing ideas and working together to solve problems.
- **Diversity and inclusivity:** A company that values diversity and inclusivity is one that is open to different perspectives and experiences. It is an environment where everyone feels respected and valued, regardless of their background or identity.
- **Work-life balance:** An ideal IT company recognizes the importance of work-life balance and provides its employees with the flexibility and resources to maintain a healthy balance between work and personal life.
- **Strong leadership and mentorship:** An ideal IT company has strong leaders who provide guidance and mentorship to their employees. It is an environment where employees are encouraged to learn and grow, both professionally and personally.
- **Emphasis on learning and development:** An ideal IT company invests in the learning and development of its employees. It is an environment where employees are encouraged to take on new challenges and learn new skills to advance their careers.

Overall, an ideal IT company is one that values its employees, fosters a collaborative and innovative work environment, and is committed to making a positive impact in the world.

### Tell me about yourself

**Key:**

1. **Research the interviewer and company beforehand** to understand their likely needs and challenges.
2. **Early in the interview, ask about the role's key priorities** before talking about yourself.

**Advices:**

- Start with who you are now (current level and specialization).
- Focus on skills relevant to the role rather than your life story.
- Highlight achievements with measurable impact.
- Connect your experience to the employer's needs.
- End with why you're interested in this opportunity.

**Answear:**

I’m a Full Stack Engineer with over 3 years of experience, mainly focused on React, Next.js, and Node.js. Currently, I work at XB Software, where I build and integrate UI components for large-scale frontend libraries like SVAR and DHTMLX. A big part of my role is not just development, but also improving performance, refactoring legacy code, and ensuring product quality through both automated testing with Cypress and manual QA across multiple products.

Before that, at Ars Magnum, I worked more deeply on architecture and scalability. I designed systems for managing complex UI structures like dozens of modal windows and dynamic tables, and introduced improvements that saved hundreds of development hours. Earlier at SLMax, I focused on performance optimization, SSR, authentication, and overall UX improvements—like reducing data loading time to nearly zero after the first request and significantly improving rendering efficiency.

What connects all my experience is that I focus on building scalable, maintainable frontend-heavy systems and improving both performance and developer experience. I’m comfortable working across the stack, but my strongest impact is usually on frontend architecture, optimization, and system design.

Right now, I’m looking for a role where I can work on complex frontend systems, contribute to architecture decisions, and continue improving performance and product quality at scale.

### What are your greatest strengths?

**Key:**

**Don’t talk about everything you’ve done — talk about the right things for this job, backed by real results.**

**Advices:**

- Don’t just describe yourself — **match yourself to the job’s needs**
- Prepare your **key strengths in advance** before the interview
- Always support strengths with **real examples from recent work**
- Focus on **achievements, not opinions about yourself**
- Be ready to switch examples depending on what the interviewer cares about
- Show **results and impact**, not just tasks
- Highlight traits employers value most:

- problem-solving & achievements
- intelligence & good judgment
- honesty & integrity
- teamwork & cultural fit
- communication skills
- reliability & dedication
- motivation & energy
- confidence & leadership

- Be clear, structured, and confident — no rambling or guessing

**Answer:**

My greatest strengths are problem-solving, building scalable architectures, and delivering high-quality products.

For example, at Ars Magnum, I designed centralized solutions for managing over 30 modal windows and 18 dynamic tables, which significantly improved maintainability and saved more than 300 hours of development time.

I'm also focused on performance and user experience. At SLMax, I optimized data caching, reducing loading times from 3 seconds to virtually instant after the first request, and improved SEO through SSR.

Another strength is quality ownership. At XB Software, I implemented Cypress automated testing and performed QA across multiple products, helping improve reliability and catch issues before release.

Finally, I work well in teams and enjoy sharing knowledge, which is reflected in my experience mentoring frontend students while continuing to grow as an engineer.

  <summary>What are your greatest weaknesses?</summary>

### What are your greatest weaknesses?

**TRAPS**: Beware - this is an eliminator question, designed to shorten the candidate list.  
Any admission of a weakness or fault will earn you an “A” for honesty, but an “F” for the  
Interview.

**Key:**

**Minimize weaknesses, maximize confidence, and always bring the focus back to your strengths and fit for the role.**

**Advices:**

1. Don’t reveal a weakness that affects core job performance
2. Avoid cliché fake weaknesses (like “I work too hard”)
3. Don’t sound defensive or overly negative about yourself
4. If possible, shift focus to confidence in your fit for the role
5. Emphasize both:

- - ability to do the job well
- - motivation to do it well

6. Reassure the interviewer there is no real barrier to your success
7. Alternatively, talk about preferences, not serious flaws
8. Make sure anything “negative” is either:

- - not important for the role, or
- - already improved

**PASSABLE ANSWER:** Disguise a strength as a weakness.

**Answer 1**

One thing I’ve been improving is balancing speed and perfection. Early in my career, I sometimes spent extra time refining architecture or edge cases because I wanted the solution to be as clean and maintainable as possible. I’ve learned that in a product environment, delivering value quickly and iterating based on feedback is often more important than finding the perfect solution upfront. Today I set clearer priorities, time-box technical investigations, and focus on the highest-impact improvements first.

**Answer 2**

Earlier in my career, I sometimes spent too much time solving problems independently before seeking feedback. I wanted to fully understand the problem and come back with a complete solution. Over time, I learned that involving teammates earlier often leads to better decisions and faster delivery. Now I discuss architecture and implementation approaches sooner, which improves collaboration and helps identify risks earlier.

### Tell me about something you did – or failed to do – that you now feel a little ashamed of.

Looking back, there were cases where a simpler approach would have delivered value faster. That experience taught me to focus more on business requirements, discuss trade-offs with the team earlier, and prioritize delivering value incrementally. Since then, I've become much better at balancing technical quality with delivery speed.

**Answear - old school**

I honestly don't spend much time thinking about regrets. I've found it's more productive to focus on what I can learn and improve. One habit I've developed is reviewing my work and decisions after completing a project or feature. I ask myself what went well, what could have been done better, and what I can apply next time. That approach has helped me continuously improve as an engineer and avoid repeating the same mistakes.

**Answear:**

Now I discuss architecture and implementation options earlier, gather feedback sooner, and focus on the solutions that provide the most value to the business while remaining maintainable and scalable.

### The “Silent Treatment”

**Key:**

Don't panic or over-talk — stay calm, wait briefly, then politely ask if there's anything else to add.

**TRAPS:**

Talk during the silence

**Answear:**

Like a primitive tribal mask, the Silent Treatment loses all it power to frighten you once you refuse to be intimidated. If your interviewer pulls it, keep quiet yourself for a while and then ask, with sincere politeness and not a trace of sarcasm, **“Is there anything else I can fill in on that point?”** That’s all there is to it. Whatever you do, don’t let the Silent Treatment intimidate you into talking a blue streak, because you could easily talk yourself out of the position.

### Why should I hire you?

**Key:**

Match your strengths to this role's needs with 2–4 proof points — need → experience → result.

**TRAPS:** Believe it or not, this is a killer question because so many candidates are  
unprepared for it. If you stammer or adlib you’ve blown it.

**Advices:**

- Find the company’s key needs before answering
- Match your skills directly to those needs
- Use real achievements, not general claims
- Structure answer: need → experience → result
- Focus on 2–4 strongest points only
- Be clear, confident, and concise
- Show impact and measurable results
- Position yourself as the solution, not just a candidate

**Answer**:

You should hire me because I bring strong full stack experience with a focus on building scalable, high-performance web applications using React, Next.js, and Node.js.

From what I understand, you're looking for someone who can contribute to both frontend and backend, improve system architecture, and deliver reliable, maintainable features. This is exactly what I've been doing in my recent roles.

For example, at SLMax, I improved application performance by optimizing caching so that data loads instantly after the first request, and implemented SSR to improve SEO and performance. At Ars Magnum, I designed scalable architecture solutions that simplified complex UI systems, reducing hundreds of hours of development time. And at XB Software, I improved product quality by introducing Cypress automated testing and helping stabilize multiple products.

Beyond technical skills, I focus on clean architecture, performance, and collaboration with teams to deliver practical solutions, not just code. I think that combination of engineering depth and product thinking makes me a strong match for this role.

<details>
  <summary>Aren’t you overqualified for this position?</summary>

### Aren’t you overqualified for this position?

**Key:**

Reframe experience as faster impact and long-term fit, not as a stepping stone.

**TRAPS:** One reason interviewers ask this question is to see if you’re settling for this  
position, using it merely as a stopover until something better comes along. Or they  
could be trying to gauge your level of ambition.  
If you’re too specific, i.e., naming the promotions you someday hope to win, you’ll sound  
presumptuous. If you’re too vague, you’ll seem rudderless.

**Advices:**

1. Don’t see it as rejection — treat it as a concern to address
2. Show that overqualification \= faster impact and higher value
3. Emphasize you can contribute immediately due to experience
4. Highlight transferable value (skills, systems, leadership, training others)
5. Make it clear you’re not just looking for any job, but this role specifically
6. Show long-term commitment and stability
7. Reduce concern about leaving by showing motivation beyond title/money
8. Turn “too experienced” into “ready to deliver from day one”

**Answer:**

I understand why that might be a concern, but I see it differently. My experience means I can contribute from day one without a long ramp-up period and bring strong practices in architecture, performance, and code quality that help the team move faster.

At this stage, I’m not focused on title — I’m focused on doing solid engineering work in a strong product team where I can contribute and continue growing. The role you’re offering fits very well with the kind of work I enjoy most, especially building scalable systems and improving product quality.

I’m also looking for stability and a long-term place where I can make an impact, not just a short-term step. So from my side, I see this as a strong match rather than being overqualified.

Show ambition aligned with this company — growth inside the role, not a hidden exit plan.

- Reassure that this role matches your strengths and interests
- Focus on doing excellent work in the current role first

### Describe your ideal company, location and job.

**Key:**

Describe values and work style that match this employer — avoid an unrealistic wish list.

**TRAPS:** This is often asked by an experienced interviewer who thinks you may be  
overqualified, but knows better than to show his hand by posing his objection directly.  
So he’ll use this question instead, which often gets a candidate to reveal that, indeed, he  
or she is looking for something other than the position at hand.

**Advices:**

- Match your answer to the company you are interviewing with
- Show genuine enthusiasm for their industry, product, and culture
- Avoid describing a “dream company” that sounds different from them
- Emphasize what they already offer as your ideal environment
- Highlight growth, impact, and meaningful engineering work
- Keep location flexible unless it’s a real requirement
- Avoid sounding like you’re waiting for a “better” opportunity
- Reassure them you fit their level and won’t feel out of place

**Answer:**

My ideal company is one where I can work on meaningful products with strong engineering standards, where quality, scalability, and performance really matter. I enjoy environments where I can collaborate with other engineers, take ownership of features, and contribute to both architecture and implementation.

The kind of job I enjoy most is full stack development using modern technologies like React, Next.js, and Node.js, where I can solve real product problems and improve system performance and maintainability.

In terms of location, I’m flexible. What matters most to me is working in a strong team, learning from others, and contributing long-term to a product I can be proud of. From what I understand about your company, this role aligns very well with that kind of environment.

Turn the “gap” into a planned, positive, and strategic decision, and show you were selective rather than inactive.

- Show you made a deliberate choice to be selective
- Emphasize self-reflection and skill improvement during the gap
- Avoid blaming previous employer or market too much
- Keep tone calm, confident, and forward-looking
- End with strong motivation to find the right long-term fit

At the same time, the market has been quite competitive, so I wanted to be selective and make sure the next step is the right long-term match for both sides. That’s why I’m particularly interested in opportunities like this one, where I can contribute and grow meaningfully.”

### Tell me honestly about the strong points and weak points of your boss (company, management team, etc.)…

**TRAPS:** Skillfull interviewers sometimes make it almost irresistible to open up and air a  
little dirty laundry from your previous position. DON’T

Never criticize previous bosses or companies — always stay positive, show professionalism, and demonstrate loyalty.

**Advice:**

- Never speak negatively about a boss, team, or company
- Don’t try to be “honest” by exposing problems — it backfires
- Interviewer is testing your **loyalty and attitude**, not your opinion of others
- Focus on what you learned or appreciated instead
- Show you handle situations professionally, even if not perfect
- Keep tone respectful, neutral, and mature
- Demonstrate that you don’t gossip or blame others

**Answer:**

I’ve been fortunate to work with teams and managers who were focused on delivering good products and improving engineering practices. In general, I appreciated the emphasis on collaboration, learning, and solving real technical problems.

Like in any environment, there are always different working styles and priorities, but I’ve found it more productive to focus on what I can learn from each situation and how I can contribute effectively, rather than judging or criticizing.

Overall, my experience has helped me grow a lot as an engineer, especially in areas like architecture, performance optimization, and teamwork.”

### On a scale of one to ten, rate me as an interviewer.

**Key:**

Never give a low score — stay respectful; commit only after the interview ends.

**TRAPS:** Give a perfect “10,” and you’ll seem too easy to please. Give anything less  
than a perfect 10, and he could press you as to where you’re being critical, and that road  
leads **downhill** for you.

**BEST ANSWER:** Once again, **never be negative.** The interviewer will only resent  
criticism coming from you. This is the time to show your positivism.

However, don’t give a numerical rating. Simply praise whatever interview style he’s been  
Using.

If he’s been tough, say “You have been thorough and tough-minded, the very qualities  
needed to conduct a good interview.”

If he’s been methodical, say, “You have been very methodical and analytical, and I’m  
sure that approach results in excellent hires for your firm.”

In other words, pay him a sincere compliment **that he can believe** because it’s anchored  
in the behavior you’ve just seen.
